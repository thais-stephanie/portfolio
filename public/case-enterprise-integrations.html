Through Workato, an enterprise integration platform, I integrated Pipefy with more than 40 systems, including SAP ECC and S/4HANA, Salesforce, NetSuite, Workday, ServiceNow and Snowflake. Those integrations sat inside live purchase, supplier and approval processes, so every change had to reach production without stopping them.

01 · The role

The same product, seen from the vendor side

I came to Pipefy after working with it from two other sides. Earlier in my career I worked in post-sales and then pre-sales at Profectum, a process-automation consultancy in Fortaleza, Brazil, that implemented Pipefy for its clients. Later, Pipefy ran the internal operations of Globalfy, a U.S. platform that helps international founders open and run American companies, and I built integrations around it there. At Pipefy itself I was on the vendor side, with enterprise customers whose processes depended on Pipefy exchanging data with their ERP, CRM and HR systems.

The job had two halves. After the sale, I owned the technical side of customer implementations and integrations. When consulting ownership of an account changed, I took over its existing integrations, investigated undocumented behavior, resolved failures and adapted the workflows to changing requirements. I managed API keys, service accounts and secrets per customer, held process reviews with recurring customers and assessed new Pipefy features against their workflows.

Before the sale, I joined Account Executives in enterprise evaluations. Section 07 covers that part.

03 · Releases

Releases that could not interrupt production

The integrations I maintained ran inside processes customers used every day, such as purchase requests that had to reach SAP. An update that broke a field mapping would stop real requests, so I released every change in stages with Workato's Recipe Lifecycle Management.

  1. 1
    Build in development. I built and validated recipes in a development environment first.
  2. 2
    Import into test. I packaged the recipes with Recipe Lifecycle Management and imported them into a test environment with its own connections and credentials for the customer's homologation systems.
  3. 3
    Test against realistic scenarios. I checked field mappings, authentication, payloads and endpoint behavior. In some projects the tests ran automatically in the delivery pipeline; in others I triggered the test suites myself.
  4. 4
    Promote only approved versions. An approved version went to production, and I monitored its jobs after the release.
  5. 5
    Keep a way back. Every change had a recovery plan in case it broke the integration.

This describes the GPA integrations and similar Pipefy projects. I can't confirm that every customer workspace had all three environments, and the delivery pipelines I worked in were built by others.

04 · Troubleshooting

When test and production disagreed

Most of the problems I debugged came from differences between environments. A 401 or a 403 can have more than one cause, so I treated the HTTP status as the starting point and confirmed the cause with logs, the connection configuration and a direct test against the endpoint.

A production SAP configured differently

During homologation at GPA, I found that the customer's SAP production configuration differed from the test environment. The same recipe had to use different endpoints and credentials in each environment without ever sending a real transaction to the wrong one. I compared the connections, adjusted the environment-specific parameters and validated the calls before publishing.

A 401 Unauthorized in validation

In one release, a recipe reached the HTTP call to SAP and returned 401. I followed the job up to that call and compared the connections. It was using the wrong environment's credentials. I corrected the connection, reran the tests and confirmed the endpoint response before approving the deployment.

403 Forbidden, only in production

Some integrations passed in test and failed in production with 403. A 403 means the request was authenticated and then refused, so I inspected the request and the logs, compared the authorization context between environments and worked with the customer's IT to correct policies and permissions on their side.

A payload change that stopped records

One update changed the format of a payload, and expected records stopped being created in the customer's ERP. I compared payloads from before and after the change, found the incompatible field mapping, fixed the transformation, tested realistic scenarios and published a validated version.

05 · SAP inside the customer's network

Reaching an SAP that was not exposed to the cloud

GPA's SAP sat inside the corporate network, and the integration had to work without bypassing the customer's access controls. The customer's infrastructure team installed and administered an agent inside that network.

I configured and managed the Workato connections that went through the agent, aligned installation, network and security requirements with their IT, waited for their approvals and tested SAP calls with their technical team. When something failed, we investigated together.

SAP ECC ↔ Pipefy

Supplier master data

SAP ECC was the system of record, and Pipefy kept a local supplier database for its request forms. I built the Workato layer between them. Scheduled recipes pre-synchronized suppliers and cost centers.

When a requested supplier was missing from the local copy, a recipe looked it up in SAP through REST and HTTP services exposed by the customer's SAP team and saved it under the SAP vendor code. I owned the recipes, orchestration, field mapping and troubleshooting.

Pipefy → SAP and ERP

Purchase requests

Requesters submitted purchase requests through Pipefy. I configured Pipefy webhooks that sent those requests through Apigee to SAP and the customer's ERP, keeping those systems up to date.

Pipefy webhook → Apigee → SAP and the customer's ERP

The SAP side belonged to the customer's SAP team. I did not work on ABAP or SAP configuration, and I did not install or administer the agent, SAP, Apigee or GPA's servers.

06 · Filling the gaps between connectors

Custom connectors, HTTP actions and SQL inside recipes

Some systems had no usable Workato connector. For those I built custom connectors with the Connector SDK in Ruby, covering authentication, actions and request and response handling, and HTTP-based custom actions for APIs that had no connector at all.

Inside recipes I used SQL Transformations to join data from different sources, filter records by business rules, deduplicate, normalize values for each system and compare statuses across systems to find inconsistencies. I also published API recipes through the Workato API Platform, with API collections, clients, keys and permissions.

Receipts into accounts payable and receivable

In one customer workflow I built an OpenAI document process that extracted and validated billing fields from hundreds of receipts arriving as PDFs, images, spreadsheets and CSVs. A person reviewed the extracted fields, and the records were routed into accounts payable and receivable. About a week of work became a few hours.

10 · My role

What was mine, and what belonged to others

I owned the Workato layer of the integrations I was assigned: recipes, orchestration, field mappings, environment-specific connections and their credentials, custom connectors and HTTP actions, the staged releases and the troubleshooting when something broke. Before the sale, I owned technical discovery, demonstrations and proofs of concept.

The customer's IT owned the on-premises agent, the network, their policies and permissions, and the servers behind SAP and Apigee. The customer's SAP team owned the services I called. Account Executives owned the commercial side of new deals and expansions, and the delivery pipelines came from other teams. From this work I treat configuration per environment as part of every release, because the incidents I debugged most often came from it.
