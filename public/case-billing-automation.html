In production since Jun 2026 HubSpot · Stripe Billing · n8n · Airtable · Deel

Subscription billing that runs on its own, and stops when the numbers don't reconcile

I rebuilt the HubSpot-to-Stripe subscription lifecycle for a US healthcare staffing company as two n8n workflows, about 250 nodes between them. They create subscriptions, change prices, apply prorations and credits, swap in replacements and cancel, without anyone in Finance clicking through Stripe.

A change executes only when the ticket, the signed agreement and the live Stripe state agree. When they don't, the ticket stops with the reason and the arithmetic written on it. Finance's weekly queue went from 60 to 80 tickets to 2 to 3 exceptions, and the time that freed up went into auditing billing.

01 · The problem

The queue was full, so nobody checked what it billed

Clients hire a role, like a front desk representative or a medical biller, for 40 hours a week or for 30. Each role is one line on the client's monthly Stripe subscription. When anything changed, a Success Manager opened a HubSpot ticket, described the change in their own words and typed in the amounts. A Finance analyst then turned it into the matching Stripe change: price, partner discount, billing date, and any proration or credit.

Finance was two people, and one of them carried payroll, refunds, reimbursements, credits and compliance on top of 60 to 80 billing tickets a week. Checking the work was the first thing to go, so billing errors were usually spotted by the client.

Two incidents in May 2026 showed it. A ticket said full time while the signed agreement said a 30-hour role, and the gap was caught only after the subscription had been created. In the other, Finance set the approval to Yes and no subscription was ever created.

What I found before designing anything

  • The executor's last run was on March 25. It had been dead for eleven weeks, Finance had gone back to editing Stripe by hand, and nothing in the system reported it.
  • The intake workflow had thrown 13 errors since May 27 without a note, an alert or an email.
  • The subscription lookup took Stripe's default page of 10 and ignored has_more. A client with six active subscriptions looked like it had three, which hid $5,650 a month from the logic making billing decisions.
  • 155 Stripe product ids were hardcoded in workflow code, and 71 of them pointed at archived products.

What the audits found later

Nobody was careless. One person had five jobs, no place to record what had been agreed, and no way to tie a request to the line it was about.

03 · Architecture

One workflow decides, the other writes

A property change on the HubSpot ticket fires a webhook into the router. Every decision lives there. Every write to Stripe lives in the executor, and nothing else in the estate touches a subscription.

Workflow 1 · 118 nodes

Router: decides what should happen

  • Resolves the company, the team member, the signed agreement and the deal
  • Classifies the request into one of eight families
  • Pairs contract rows to deal line items and picks the live subscription item the change targets
  • Computes the proration, credit or final period charge
  • Writes a plain-language note on the ticket, then builds the payload for the executor or stops
Workflow 2 · 132 nodes

Executor: the only writer to Stripe

  • Re-validates state and takes an item-level lock
  • Applies the change in Stripe
  • Writes the new subscription item id back to the contract record
  • Posts a completion note and moves the ticket
  • Sends any failure to the dead letter queue with its full payload

The split gives one answer to "where do I look". A wrong amount points to the router. A right amount that never landed points to the executor. Across three months of production incidents, almost every defect could be placed on one side within minutes.

My first design generated Stripe drafts for Finance to review, which would activate after a monitoring window. On a call on June 19, Finance said they wanted the change applied at approval, with the prorated invoice finalized right away. I reworked the change path that day and dropped the activation listener I had built, about two days of work.

04 · Guardrails

Uncertain data stops the request

This system moves money, so the stop conditions were in the design from the first draft. Nothing defaults. If a client's segment can't be resolved, the request fails instead of guessing one, because a guess hands Finance a clean-looking result with no sign that anything was uncertain.

Idempotency in the application

Stripe's keys expire in about 24 hours

The monitoring window was two weeks, so a retry on day three had no native protection. Keys live on the ticket. Item-level locks stop two writes to the same subscription item. A payload checksum catches any change between approval and execution, and a consumed flag keeps one approval from firing twice.

Hard stops

retrying bad data doesn't make it safe

The request stops when the ticket and the signed agreement disagree, when the target subscription item can't be identified, when the segment is ambiguous, when an amount doesn't reconcile, or when the payload changed after approval. The ticket gets a named reason and the arithmetic, and goes back for correction.

Money in whole cents

integer arithmetic, business days

Prices, prorations and credits are calculated in integer cents. Prorations count business days with Finance's fixed divisor of 21, excluding the billing date itself, and period boundaries are read in the business time zone.

A recovery path people can see

dead letter queue with aging

Failed items keep their full original payload. An aging watcher surfaces the ones left unresolved, a stuck-ticket alert catches requests that stop moving, a reconciler heals orphaned states, and a global error handler covers the rest.

One field, two meanings

Success Managers fill in a fee field on the ticket. Some enter the amount before the partner discount and some after it. In one week, two clients were billed from that field, one gross and one net, with nothing telling them apart.

Declaring a convention would have broken every historical ticket and moved real money the first time someone got it wrong. So the resolver tests both readings against a second anchor (the catalog price, the deal line item, or the price of the subscription being replaced) and accepts whichever reconciles to the cent. When neither does, the request stops.

05 · Monitoring and reconciliation

Two separate checks: did it run, and is the billing right

The old executor had stopped running for eleven weeks and nothing in the system could say so. The rebuild checks two things on separate paths. Monitoring covers whether each step ran: the global error handler, the dead letter queue and its aging watcher. Reconciliation covers whether Stripe matches the contract, and it rebuilds that answer from scratch.

The auditor I built reconstructs the expected mapping between each contract and its subscription item, then compares it with what the system stored.

06 · What broke

Four failures and what each one changed

I numbered defects as I found them. The count reached 188 in about three months. These four taught me the most.

A run reported success and charged nothing

I built a Stripe invoice call with a parameter combination the API rejects. The failure went to the dead letter queue as it should, but the item status map was assembled from the wrong input reference and reported every item as succeeded. Five invoice items totaling $832.71 sat stranded, and the ticket closed itself.

It had the same shape as the founding incident, an approval that produced nothing. A status now has to come from what actually executed.

Part of a client's billing was invisible

The subscription lookup relied on Stripe's default page size and ignored has_more. A client with six active subscriptions looked like it had three, so $5,650 a month of its billing never reached the decision logic.

I treat a list response as partial until the API says it is complete.

The matcher picked the wrong item

A write-once latch let the first subscription item that matched any signal win, even when a later one matched on stronger evidence. One client's credits were computed against a $2,250 item instead of the correct $2,418 one.

The fix was small, but it touched every subscription, so I ran a census first: 815 of 869 subscriptions were provably unchanged, and across 1,006 replayed cases accuracy went from 89.46% to 100%, with no item moving to a wrong one. The census took longer than the fix.

Finance caught one I missed

The estimator ran once per ticket when it needed to run once per subscription. A batch covering three team members across two subscriptions with different billing dates was prorated as one: the estimate said $559.29, and the correct figure was $832.71.

Finance found it in the review note during the observation period. An auditor with time catches that; an operator in a 70-ticket queue doesn't.

07 · Testing and rollout

From tested to fully automatic, in steps

  • Offline Node test harnesses replayed the published node source against real ticket payloads before every change.
  • Every publish was gated on a byte-level checksum of the node source, after the n8n editor once saved six page-up keystrokes as text inside a code node.
  • Any change that touched the whole portfolio got a blast-radius census before it shipped.
  • In the second monitoring week, all eight issues that came up were diagnosed, fixed, published and confirmed with Finance in the same cycle.
  • Some request types launched behind a Finance approval flag as a monitoring period. The window came back clean, the flag came off, and every request type, cancellations included, now applies automatically.
08 · Results

Finance went from operating billing to auditing it

The weekly queue fell from 60 to 80 tickets to 2 to 3. Those are the hard stops, by design; everything else executes and reports. Finance made auditing a standing activity, month-end close improved, overtime came down, and the team didn't have to grow.

The time that freed up made portfolio audits possible. A Python parser indexed 1,168 signed agreements, and the first sweep compared them with 830 live subscriptions in Stripe. It produced 157 findings, netting to $13,467.98 a month of contracted revenue that wasn't being billed. After review, ten clients stayed on their earlier agreements, and the remaining amount was approved and put into billing.

09 · My role

What was mine, and what was Finance's

I was responsible and accountable for the architecture and the build. I designed the two-workflow model, wrote the design documents, built and published both workflows, and wrote the audit tooling, the reconciliation scripts and the test harnesses. I also ran the production incident loop and kept the decision log. A design partner reviewed architecture decisions with me, and a leadership sponsor approved scope.

Every pricing rule, proration formula, credit policy and approval threshold belongs to Finance. I wrote each one down, marked what they had confirmed apart from what I was assuming, and built only what was confirmed. Before building, I sent Finance 26 open questions and paused until they came back. When two answers contradicted each other, I went back and asked.

10 · What I'd do differently

What I'd do differently

Scope idempotency to the ticket from the start. Item-scoped keys let one request create three invoices, two of them empty, which then failed.

Ship the audit layer with the first deploy. The whole point was to make Finance the auditor, yet the audit tools lagged the automation by weeks, while cancellations were already running with no human in the loop.

Time the old process. I know the queue size because Finance told me. I can't give minutes per ticket, because nobody timed it and I didn't ask early enough.

Next, the problem moves upstream. Most remaining defects start where the deal is closed, and a fact entered wrong there can't be fixed downstream. The queued design treats deal closure as the trigger, reads the signed quote, and creates one fulfillment ticket per line item instead of one per deal.
