Subscription billing, rebuilt so Finance could audit it instead of operating it

A healthcare staffing company places professionals with clients and bills each placement as a line on a monthly subscription. Every time a client's team changed, the bill had to change with it. A two person Finance team handled that on top of payroll, refunds, reimbursements, credits and compliance, and billing alone meant 60 to 80 tickets a week. With that load, checking the work was the first thing to go, so billing errors were usually spotted by the client.

01 · How a billing change used to happen

Clients here don't buy software seats. They hire a role: a front desk representative, a medical biller, someone for 40 hours a week or for 30. Each role is one line on the client's monthly subscription.

Each client works with a Success Manager. When something changed, the Success Manager opened a ticket for Finance and described it in their own words: add a role, give someone a raise, go from 40 hours to 30, pause a line while someone is being replaced, cancel. They also typed in the amounts. A Finance analyst then turned that description into the matching change in Stripe, with the right price, the right discount, the right billing date, and any partial-month charge or credit.

Where it broke

Two things made that harder than it sounds.

A lot was agreed in conversation. A Success Manager could settle a specific number of weekly hours with a client, a discount outside the standard price list, or a raise for someone. Not all of it made it back into the record.

The ticket described the change by amount, but never said which billing line it meant. If a client had three similar roles at similar rates, and none of the amounts in the ticket matched what was being billed, Finance couldn't tell which line to change. The analyst went back to the Success Manager, and the Success Manager went digging through old emails and agreements to rebuild what had been promised.

On top of that, the same person was running the queue and checking it, so every hour spent on one came out of the other. In May 2026 a ticket said full time while the signed agreement said a 30 hour role. It was only caught after the subscription had been created.

03 · How it works now

The Success Manager still opens the same ticket. The difference is what happens next: before anything touches billing, the ticket gets checked.

01
The request is identified and checked
The first workflow works out what kind of change it is, finds the signed agreement and the exact billing line involved, and compares them. If they disagree, it stops and writes on the ticket exactly what doesn't match.
02
Only one place can change billing
The second workflow is the only thing allowed to write to Stripe. After making the change, it reads Stripe back to confirm the result is what it intended.
03
The ticket closes with a receipt
It says what was applied. Anything that failed goes to a list Finance can see, showing how long each item has been waiting.

04 · What it handles on its own
New client subscription Adding a role Raises and price increases Price reductions, like fewer hours Partial-month charges and credits Discounts and card fees Credits carried to the next invoice Removing a role Swapping in a replacement Cancellation
No double charges
idempotency and locking
If a ticket is retried, it can't charge twice, and two requests on the same subscription can't run at the same time.
Failures stay visible
dead letter queue with aging
Anything that fails goes to a list showing how long it has been waiting, and someone resolves it. Nothing keeps retrying quietly in the background.
Money in whole cents
integer arithmetic
Every price, partial-month charge and credit is calculated in whole cents, so rounding can't drift. Billing dates respect business days and time zones.
Nothing ships untested
checksum gated deployment
Every change is replayed offline against real past requests first, and what gets deployed is checked to be exactly what was tested.
05 · Comparing what clients signed with what they pay

Automating the queue was half the job. The other half was a comparison nobody had had time to make: what each client signed against what they were actually being charged. A Python script read 1,168 signed agreements, a reconciliation step compared them with live billing in Stripe, and every mismatch went into a findings list that Finance works through.

07 · What I'd flag to the next owner

The approval step was temporary. A few request types launched with a Finance approval step so the first weeks could be watched closely. That period ended, and by September 2026 every request type, cancellations included, applied automatically.

The weak spot is where the agreement gets made. When something a Success Manager agrees with a client never reaches the record, the automation can flag the mismatch, but it can't know what was promised. The next thing worth building is a structured place to capture those agreements when they happen.

The rules came from Finance. I wrote each one down, marked what Finance had confirmed apart from what I was assuming, and only built what had been confirmed. When two answers contradicted each other, I went back and asked.
